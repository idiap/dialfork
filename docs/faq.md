## What about tool calls?

Procedural hallucination is a third axis, alongside factual accuracy and correct tool use. Checking tool calls and checking the conversation look at different sides of the same agent, and each catches failures the other can't see:

| | **the conversation follows the procedure** | **the conversation drifts** |
|---|---|---|
| **tool calls are right** | ✅ all good | the forecast is fetched but never told; the right flight is booked after three pitches for insurance you declined |
| **tool calls are wrong** | "Let me check that for you", then the wrong tool, or none | both sides go wrong |

Tool checks see the bottom row; DialFork looks at the right column, the side users see and interact with.

**Most steps of a procedure are conversational.** In the weather example of the README, only one step of four is a tool call. The same holds for most procedures people follow in conversation, and for some, almost every step is:

- a bank assistant has to verify who you are *before* telling you your balance;
- a tutor has to guide the student to the answer, not give it away;
- an interviewer or a survey has to ask the same questions in the same order;
- a support agent has to ask for confirmation before acting, and give the disclosures it must give.

None of these steps is a tool call, and an agent can skip or reorder them while every call it makes is right.

**The conversation often drifts first.** In the README example, the agent goes off into a survey at its very first reply; a check on tool calls would only notice that the forecast was never fetched, if at all, once the conversation is over. Catching the drift in the conversation, turn by turn, is what [Task 3](task3.md) is about: a guardrail can step in before the agent acts on it.

## The dialogues are generated by an LLM. Do the labels mean anything?

The labels come from how the dialogues are built, so we checked them with people: [30 annotators judged 428 conversations](human_study.md). They confirmed the injected behaviour in 91% of forks, placed 77% of onsets within one turn of the fork turn, and their blind ratings follow the fork order (Kendall τ<sub>b</sub> = 0.64). An [LLM judge](llm_judge.md) calibrated on their judgments reproduces these findings on the whole corpus.

## Can't a method just spot where a fork stops copying its original?

Only partly, and it's a shortcut we measure. The *shared prefix* control, which counts the opening turns two dialogues share word for word, reaches τ = 0.68 on [Task 2](task2.md); FlowScore beats it with every encoder (τ 0.81–0.85). The replayed opening is not always verbatim either, and real dialogues don't come with a conformant twin to compare against. [DialFork v2](../README.md#-roadmap) will paraphrase the opening, so that this shortcut stops working.

## Do I need the workflow?

No. [Tasks 1](task1.md) and [2](task2.md) compare dialogues with each other, without any workflow. In [Task 3](task3.md), streaming detectors learn what conformant dialogues look like from past ones; giving an LLM the ground-truth workflow is a separate regime, reported for reference, since you rarely have it in practice.

## Is DialFork only about hallucination?

No. The forks give a ground truth for how differently conversations unfold, which is useful beyond hallucination: comparing generated or simulated dialogues with real ones, or an agent before and after a model upgrade ([Task 1](task1.md)), and finding or clustering conversations that went the same way ([Task 2](task2.md)).
